Sooooooooooo.... I just graduated high school.

Writing about a three year period of your life, and how it led you to the next one, has proven to be difficult in a way I didn't expect. It's easy to list what happened. It's much harder to explain how it felt, or why any of it mattered, or what I was actually thinking in the moments that turned out to matter most. Along the way I met some incredible people, took on things I genuinely didn't believe I could pull off when I first started high school, and somehow kept my grades intact through all of it.

All of that led me to the Software Engineering program at the University of Waterloo, one of the most sought-after technology programs in the country. But this post isn't really about that acceptance letter. It's as much about my thoughts and perspectives on my time in high school as it is about the steps I took to get there, and about the unexpected opportunities I stumbled into along the way, some of which had nothing to do with technology at all.

# Where I Started (Grade 9) - 2022/23

My love for programming goes back further than high school. I got into web development during the COVID pandemic in 2020, the way a lot of bored, locked-down kids picked up hobbies that year, and before Grade 9 I'd already built a few small things: an early version of what would become [CoinDeno](https://github.com/soodaayush/coindeno), a handful of [Frontend Mentor challenges](https://github.com/soodaayush/frontend-mentor-challenges). It wasn't a plan. It was just something to do that happened to also be fun, and it slowly turned into the thing that would end up shaping most of what came after.

Most of my middle school years were swallowed by COVID and the lockdowns that came with it, so there weren't many initiatives to throw myself into early on. A lot of that time went into two very different things: Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu at my local [gym](https://titansmaaf.ca/), one of the only places that still felt normal, and, on the completely opposite end of productive, playing [Among Us](https://store.steampowered.com/app/945360/Among_Us/) with my friends for what felt like entire days at a stretch. It made zero sense outside of a lockdown, but at the time it was exactly the kind of dumb, low-stakes thing that kept things feeling normal when nothing else did.

In Grade 9, I genuinely wasn't thinking about university. I went to school, studied, had fun, came home, played video games, tinkered with whatever project I was on. That was it. That was the whole plan, if you could call it one.

Then, sometime in the middle of the year, conversations started creeping in about what people wanted to do after high school, where they wanted to go, what they wanted to study. It felt abstract to me, almost like eavesdropping on a conversation meant for someone older, but it planted something. I started researching universities in Canada for Computer Science and Software Engineering, and a handful of names kept coming up: [Dalhousie](https://www.dal.ca/), [McGill](https://www.mcgill.ca/), [Waterloo](https://uwaterloo.ca/), [UofT](https://www.utoronto.ca/), [UBC](https://www.ubc.ca/).

Waterloo stood out for one specific reason: its co-op program. I liked the idea of industry over research at the time, and the idea of alternating between classes and real jobs, while still in school, felt like exactly the kind of program built for someone like me. The challenge was that Waterloo's [CS](https://uwaterloo.ca/future-students/programs/computer-science) and [SWE](https://uwaterloo.ca/future-students/programs/software-engineering) programs weren't easy to get into. You needed good grades, real programming experience, and a resume of extracurriculars. At the time I had decent grades and a bit of programming experience. Extracurriculars, I had none.

So I started thinking, as early as Grade 9, about how to actually build toward this. I'm honestly not sure a 14 year old is supposed to be thinking that strategically about anything, let alone a university application four years out, but at the time it felt less like ambition and more like the only thing I actually knew how to do with the uncertainty. One of the more obvious first steps was Waterloo's math contests, so I decided on writing the [Pascal](https://cemc.uwaterloo.ca/contests/pcf) Contest that year. I prepared for weeks, wrote it at my school, and came away with a score that was above average, but was somewhat underwhelming. I remember feeling a little deflated by it at the time. I'd put in real hours, and it didn't feel like enough to show for it. It was probably the first time I learned that effort doesn't always show up in the score, and it definitely wasn't the last.

Around the same time, bored and looking for something to fill the last stretch of middle school, I found something I was genuinely reluctant to try: [Improv](https://www.improv.ca/). Improv is acting without a script, which sounds fun until you realize it means being funny, or interesting, or at least not silent, in front of people, with zero preparation. I avoided public speaking at all costs before this. Every single meeting, I was nervous right up until the moment I actually had to perform in front of even a small crowd of my peers. But something about the low-stakes, playful chaos of it wore that fear down over time. By the end, I'd made the competitive team, and we won first place at the Halifax regional competition, one of my first real wins at anything. Looking back, joining improv might be the single best decision I made in those years, not because of the trophy, but because of its influence on boosting my overall confidence. In the process I made some cool friends whom I'm still friends with to this day.

At the end of the year, I graduated middle school. I was proud of what I'd started to get involved in, but still had no real sense of what I wanted high school to be. I just knew, standing at the edge of it, that I needed to make the next three years count.

# Finding My Niche (Grade 10) - 2023/24

Courses:
- Drama 10
- English 10
- Mathematics 10
- Science 10
- Yoga 11
- Business Technology 11
- Communications Technology 11

Before starting high school, I sat down and wrote myself a rough mental note of how I could work toward getting into Waterloo:

- Math contests (Euclid, etc.)
- Coding contests
- Side projects (GitHub, open source)
- Regular coding
- High grades (95%+)
- Extracurriculars
- Strength across Math, English, Science, and Computer Science

Making that list felt like two things at once: exciting, because I finally had something resembling a plan, and overwhelming, because it was a lot.

I wasn't sure how I'd actually juggle all of it, and for the first few months of Grade 10, I felt a little lost. That was also the year I realized coasting through tests without studying was no longer going to cut it, so I built myself an actual framework. Nothing complicated: pay attention in class, ask questions the moment something didn't click, grind through homework both at school and after, and start studying at least a week ahead of every test. It helped that I wasn't yet juggling extracurriculars that ate into my evenings, so I had the room to actually practice this until it stuck.

On my first day of high school, I noticed Student Council putting out a call for help with events. I applied on the spot with a letter of intent, got in, and my advisor picked up on something in that letter: I'd leaned hard into my passion for technology. It turned out the school needed a [Technology Club](https://www.instagram.com/bvhstechclub/), and she thought I might be the one to start it.

I realized this could be the extracurricular that defined my entire application, something that let me take what I actually loved and turn it into something the school community could use. But I hesitated, longer than I'd like to admit. I had no experience running a meeting, let alone a club, and I kept quietly punting the idea week after week. Eventually I just forced myself to start, disorganized or not, because the alternative was never starting at all.

I settled on a simple mission statement:

> "Exploring tech together, making it fun and accessible for everyone in our school"

We'd meet every Wednesday, explore a technical concept together, and get our hands dirty with something practical. Going into that first meeting, I was genuinely nervous nobody would actually show up. I'd spread the word to as many people as I could, including a handful of friends who promised they'd pull up no matter what, and they did, which meant the room wasn't empty even before anyone else walked in. For the first year I focused on web development, my first love, since it let people see the results of their work immediately instead of waiting weeks to feel like they'd built something real. Within a month, we had a consistent turnout of 30 people, which nobody at our school had ever seen from a club before. I remember being genuinely stunned by it, standing at the front of a full classroom that I'd expected to be half-empty, and that surprise is what kept me teaching web development for the rest of the year.

My guidance counsellors ran a Google Classroom where job and volunteering opportunities were posted, and it was there that I first saw a form for the Nova Scotia Minister of Education's Student Advisory Council, a group that would meet with the Minister every few months to talk through issues in the provincial education system. I applied almost on a whim, not expecting much, and a couple of months later I was accepted and invited to my first meeting.

That first meeting covered real, live policy questions: whether to ban cellphones during class time, whether a universal lunch program made sense provincially, changes to the math curriculum, how exam periods should be structured. Sitting at that table as a Grade 10 student, listening to people debate decisions that would actually affect thousands of students across the province, was the first time I understood that "getting involved" could mean something more than joining a club.

That same period, I was also preparing for the [Canadian Computing Competition](https://cemc.uwaterloo.ca/contests/ccc) (Junior Division), the [Cayley](https://cemc.uwaterloo.ca/contests/pcf), and the [Galois](https://cemc.uwaterloo.ca/contests/fgh). For CCC, I ground through past contests, got comfortable in Python, and simulated real test conditions when I practiced. For the math contests, I leaned into the last few questions specifically, since those test concepts you won't find anywhere in the standard curriculum. CCC went well, well enough that I received a Certificate of Distinction and was placed on [Honour Rolls](https://cemc.uwaterloo.ca/sites/default/files/documents/2024/2024CCCResults.pdf) for the contest. The math contests were a different story. After weeks of prep, seeing results that didn't really reflect the work I'd put in was genuinely disappointing in the moment. But I told myself that if I locked in harder the following year, I could do better, and left it at that.

Near the end of the year, Student Council opened applications for executive roles. As a general member, I'd spent the year helping executives run event logistics. Being an executive meant more responsibility, and more freedom to actually make changes, which appealed to me a lot. One role in particular caught my eye: Tech Representative, responsible for production tech at assemblies, football games, musicals, and everything in between. Given the club I was already running and where my interests naturally sat, it felt almost too obvious. I applied, interviewed, and got the role for Grade 11.

At the year-end club awards ceremony, I was recognized for starting the Tech Club and its early success, which felt like a small but real confirmation that the risk of starting something from nothing had actually paid off.

That summer, I got into a math camp at Dalhousie, where I got a small glimpse into what university life would look like while learning advanced Mathematical concepts such as Topology, Cosmology, Calculus, and Probability. I also took a volunteer camp counsellor position at the [Canada Games Centre](https://canadagamescentre.ca/), and completed Harvard's [CS50x](https://pll.harvard.edu/course/cs50-introduction-computer-science), building a [mobile app](https://github.com/soodaayush/BVHApp) for my school as the final project. 

Grade 10 mattered because it's the year I found a sense of direction, even if I didn't fully realize it at the time. Grade 11 is where the grind really accelerates, and where balancing academics, extracurriculars, and an actual social life stops being optional and starts being a skill you have to build on purpose.

# Building Momentum (Grade 11) - 2024/25

Courses:
- Advanced Biology 11
- Advanced English 11
- Advanced Physics 11
- Canadian History 11
- Chemistry 11
- Electrotechnologies 11
- Mathematics 11
- Pre-Calculus 11

Grade 11 hit differently from day one. Suddenly I went from one science course to physics, biology, and chemistry running simultaneously, on top of pre-calculus, English, and electives. The course load alone would have been enough of an adjustment. It wasn't the only one. Between clubs, competitions, and a rapidly growing list of commitments, this was the year everything started running in parallel instead of one thing at a time.

I joined Peer Tutoring early that year, a club where students are available daily to tutor their peers on everything from math to the arts. I wanted to sharpen my own tutoring skills, so I dedicated time throughout the year to helping students with math and science. It turned out to be genuinely fulfilling, not just for the students I worked with, but for my own understanding of the material. Explaining something forces you to actually know it.

The Tech Representative role also had me handling production tech at school events that year: assemblies, musicals, and football games. Football games ended up being the most entertaining part of it, since a few of us also got roped into co-announcing them, despite the fact that I had no real idea how football actually works. I still don't. I'd call touchdowns that very clearly weren't touchdowns, mix up basic rules mid-sentence, and generally just make things up with total confidence. Somehow that made it one of the more fun, lighthearted roles I had all year. There's something genuinely enjoyable about being whimsically bad at your job.

But the bulk of my year still ran through Tech Club. It had a strong first year, and I wanted to do more than just sustain that. I wanted to build on it, run our own events, grow it into something bigger. But almost immediately I ran into a wall: I couldn't do it alone anymore. Between the academic load ahead of me and the scale of what I was trying to build, carrying the whole thing on my own effort just wasn't sustainable. So I created an executive team, people who could actually share the load with me. When I opened applications, the response surprised me. There were people who genuinely wanted to help run things, not just show up and watch. Some of the closest friendships I made in high school came out of that team, some of it through just being around each other constantly, but a lot of it through the shared chaos of scrambling to fix something twenty minutes before an event started. That team changed everything. Setup time for events dropped from 30 minutes to 10 with extra hands on deck. More people meant more ideas, more varied workshops, more of the club feeling like it belonged to everyone rather than just me. We started running events purely to raise our profile and fundraise: a Mario Kart tournament, a Hamilton movie night.

Running events with a team of mostly inexperienced students taught me something I didn't expect: planning beats enthusiasm every time. Enthusiasm gets you excited about an idea. Planning is what actually gets it across the finish line. My rough process looked something like this: pick an event, lock in a date at least three weeks out, let people choose the roles they actually want (food, advertising, whatever), finish the logistics five days ahead, have an actual plan for setup and teardown on the day itself, and afterward, sit down and reflect on what worked and what didn't. It never went exactly to plan. People get busy, things slip, and that's fine, as long as the framework keeps things from fully falling apart.

That year we also decided to compete outside our own walls, entering the Lockheed Martin [CYBERQUEST](/blog/lockheed-martin-cyberquest-2025) cybersecurity competition, a Capture the Flag event where teams work through hacking challenges against the clock, and the [Atlantic Canada AI Summit Youth AI Pitch Competition](/blog/atlantic-canada-ai-summit-youth-ai-pitch-2025), where our team designed and pitched an AI concept to improve the experience of families at the IWK hospital's Emergency Department. None of us had ever competed in anything like this before, and we went in without really knowing what to expect. We ended up placing 3rd in CYBERQUEST and 2nd in the pitch competition, results that genuinely surprised all of us. I wrote separate blog posts on both if you want the full story of how we got there.

The biggest event of that year was Hamilton Night, an after-school movie night where we invited the whole school to watch the musical. Over 40 people showed up, we raised some money for the club, and somewhere in the middle of it I found myself watching a room full of my classmates belting out songs from a musical I'd genuinely never seen before that night. It's a strange, specific kind of joy, watching people you know lose themselves in something together. That night is one of my favourite memories from the whole club.

Club meetings that year leaned further into web development, Python, and cybersecurity through [PicoCTF](https://cylabacademy.org/) (now CyLab Security Academy), and attendance held steady the entire year, which felt like proof the club had staying power beyond its first exciting months.

Outside of Tech Club, I found myself pulled into an entirely different kind of work that year. I joined the Nova Scotia Secondary School Students' Association [(NSSSA)](https://www.nsssa.ca/) as Public Relations Manager for the Halifax region, a non-profit, student-led organization that runs leadership conferences across the province. I had no real background in outreach, and it turned out the job mostly meant cold emailing companies at volume for sponsorships. It was a slow, unglamorous grind, and I learned most of what I know about persistence from it. First you find companies worth emailing, using whatever tools you can (AI, the internet, whatever works). Then you send the emails and get ignored, or rejected, over and over, at a rate that would discourage most people. I'd email a dozen companies and maybe hear back from one or two. It taught me a lesson close to job hunting: results come down to reps, even if volume alone isn't the whole story. The bigger thing it taught me, looking back, is that cold-emailing can open doors you genuinely don't expect, even when most of what you send disappears into silence. I didn't think a handful of sponsorship emails would lead anywhere close to what they eventually did, and I've carried that lesson into almost everything since: you rarely know in advance which email, which application, which cold reach-out is going to be the one that actually goes somewhere. Eventually it clicked, and we secured enough sponsorship to run a full conference. That role also led to a referral for IT Coordinator the following year, managing Azure infrastructure and support tickets, territory I already knew well.

That same year, some of that effort circled back in a way I didn't see coming. I was awarded the Nova Scotia Lieutenant Governor's [Education Medal](https://lt.gov.ns.ca/honours-awards/awards), given to two Grade 11 students per school for leadership, academic excellence, and community service. The application was fairly simple on paper (applicant details, an essay, a resume), but getting the news back felt like something bigger than the process suggested. It landed like real validation, proof that everything I'd been quietly building toward actually meant something to someone outside my own head. I met the Honourable Mike Savage, Lieutenant Governor of Nova Scotia, at the ceremony, along with his staff.

On the contest side, I also wrote the senior division of CCC that February. The jump in difficulty from junior was significant, so I ground through old problem sets again and, this time, added [Codeforces](https://codeforces.com/) contests into the mix for their notoriously hard problems that require Mathematical intuition. I scored above average with the contest, receiving a Certificate of Distinction.

That spring, I also went to my first ever Mascot Showdown, a Halifax tradition where mascots from rival schools face off while their students cheer them on, and the school with the loudest crowd effectively wins. I went just as a spectator, not expecting much out of it. Bay View ended up taking the win that year, and I ended up having a genuinely great time watching the whole chaotic thing unfold, enough that it stuck with me as one of my favourite nights of the year, and enough that I'd end up chasing that feeling again from the other side of it the following year.

Around the same time, I found another way to put what I knew to use, this time as a teacher rather than a competitor. That spring, I applied for and was selected as a Section Leader for Stanford University's [Code in Place](https://codeinplace.stanford.edu/), a free six-week Python course. I led weekly one-hour sessions with a cohort of about 15 students, walking them through variables, functions, data structures, and the usual early Python fundamentals. I met some genuinely sharp people through it, and my own understanding of the material sharpened right alongside theirs. It was the first time I really understood, in a felt way and not just as a saying, that teaching is one of the best ways to learn something yourself.

That momentum carried straight into the summer. I applied for and got into [Shad](https://www.shad.ca/), a month-long STEAM and entrepreneurship enrichment program hosted at universities across the country. I spent it living on campus with a team of strangers, working through a real-world design and entrepreneurship problem alongside people from all over Canada. This was my second time gaining insight into university life and I genuinely believe it prepared me immensely for my future studies at Waterloo. I won't go into the full experience here, since I [wrote](/blog/shad-unb-2025) about it separately, but it was one of the more formative months of my life. 

I also got into the University of Waterloo's Quantum School for Young Students [(QSYS)](https://uwaterloo.ca/institute-for-quantum-computing/outreach/qsys), a nine-day program with the Institute for Quantum Computing, held virtually that year. I was one of 200 students selected nationwide, learning concepts like quantum entanglement and cryptography that were genuinely hard to wrap my head around. The material fought back, but the staff and the peers around me made it worth pushing through anyway.

By the time August rolled around, the summer's momentum shifted toward something more serious: getting ready for the actual university applications. Some schools publish their supplementary questions online; some don't. Waterloo does, both the [AIF questions](https://uwaterloo.ca/future-students/admissions/admission-information-form) and their [engineering interview questions](https://uwaterloo.ca/engineering/future-students/applying/online-interviews), and having access to those ahead of time was an enormous help in understanding what they were actually looking for. I started drafting responses that same month. Were those early drafts any good? Not really. But having something on the page, however rough, gave me real structure heading into the school year instead of walking in blind.

I also mapped out the major national scholarships I planned to apply for: [Loran](https://loranscholar.ca/), [TD Scholarships for Community Leadership](https://www.td.com/ca/en/about-td/ready-commitment/community-leadership-scholarship-for-canadians), [Schulich Leader](https://schulichleaders.com/), and [Terry Fox](https://terryfoxawards.ca/). These were, without exaggeration, some of the most competitive undergraduate scholarships in the country, each demanding its own extensive application. Knowing what was coming, I made a real point of actually resting that summer, so I'd have something left in the tank for what September was about to throw at me.

## September

This was the most important year of the whole plan, and easily the hardest academically. Three AP courses stacked on top of a full slate of advanced courses. My first semester alone had me juggling AP Chemistry and AP Literature at the same time.

I carefully managed my extracurricular load that year, knowing academics needed more of my attention than in any previous year, but I still stayed meaningfully involved rather than stepping back entirely.

The first surprise of the year was that national scholarships opened before university applications did. Loran opened in early September, and it was entirely on me to track when things opened and closed. University applications followed in mid-to-late September. Alongside Waterloo, I also applied to Dalhousie, UBC, McGill, and UofT, more or less the same five schools that had first come up in my research back in Grade 9. For Ontario specifically, that meant going through the Ontario Universities' Application Centre [(OUAC)](https://www.ouac.on.ca/) rather than applying to schools directly. My best advice from that whole process is simple: check your email regularly, because that's where almost everything actually happens.

When it came to the AIF, and honestly any application essay, my approach stayed the same throughout: answer the actual question, use the full word count, and resist the urge to guess what sounds impressive. There isn't one right topic, and no topic is inherently more impressive than another. The best advice I've heard, and the advice I'll pass on, is to be genuinely yourself. You know your own story better than any admissions strategy ever will, and it's on you to connect your real experiences to the questions in a way that actually represents you. It's tempting to chase what you think admissions officers (and their AI) want to see. Resist that. Write what's actually true, and just make sure it answers the question fully within the word count. The engineering interview questions, for what it's worth, are also posted publicly on Waterloo's website. One more thing I'd recommend to anyone applying: go to UW's [info sessions](https://uwaterloo.ca/future-students/tours-events/info-sessions). They cover everything from first-year life to co-op to residence, usually run in the evenings, and they're a genuinely useful way to actually get to know the university rather than just researching it from a distance.

## October

Back at school, Tech Club stayed just as central to my year as ever. I wanted it to keep competing in my final year, so I signed us up for [CyberPatriot](https://www.uscyberpatriot.org/), where teams act as IT professionals defending simulated networks against real vulnerabilities. From October to December, my team and I met monthly at a library to run Windows and Linux virtual machines and defend them: turning on firewalls, adjusting user permissions, removing unnecessary programs, and answering networking questions on Cisco. The competition got noticeably harder as the months went on, and we didn't qualify for the final round. Still, we earned Gold Tier, the first time our school had ever competed at all, which felt like a real accomplishment given we were building this from nothing.

Outside of Tech Club, I picked up a couple of other roles that pulled me in different directions. In October, [Peer Tutoring](https://www.instagram.com/bayview_peertutors/) needed new leadership and ran an election for four co-lead positions. I ran, and was elected. My focus became increasing our visibility, in person and online, and building an executive team the same way I had for Tech Club: applications, interest, interviews, a real structure underneath the effort. It worked. That year became the most active in the program's history, with over 100 students attending our study nights and our social presence stronger than it had ever been. Leadership in a domain I wasn't as naturally confident in taught me that a strong team can carry you through unfamiliar territory, if you let it.

I also took on the mostly-for-fun role of morning announcer, reading the day's announcements over the intercom to every classroom in the school. Part of it was genuine enjoyment. Part of it was a deliberate push to keep working on public speaking, which still needed the reps. If you want a sense of what that actually sounded like, [here's a clip of my very last announcements](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1ZWjVOmbG8tb2gupPGJ_XDe9-7YAhmFIL/view?usp=sharing) before I handed the intercom off for good.

The other major thread that year ran through NSSSA and my school board. Early in the year, I began my duties as IT Coordinator for NSSSA: support tickets, permission updates, training members on Microsoft 365. When I started hearing that people thought the old NSSSA website felt outdated, and that there was appetite to rebuild it, I volunteered without really thinking it through. The leadership team was thrilled at the idea, so I spent the better part of two months building it out, at school and at home, on WordPress, a tool I hadn't used before despite having written actual website code for years. I [redesigned](https://www.nsssa.ca/) it to feel warmer, more organized, splitting what used to be one cluttered page into a proper multi-page site with real structure. Hearing from the leadership team about how excited people were when it launched at a regional conference is one of those quiet, genuinely rewarding moments that doesn't show up on a resume but stuck with me anyway.

To end off the month, I received my first university acceptance by Dalhousie!

## November

Alongside that, I also joined my school board's Regional Student Advisory Council (StAC) with the HRCE, structured similarly to MSAC: one student per school, meeting with the Regional Executive Director. We only met twice, but the conversations, around social media safety and how the board handled snow days, were real ones.

Meanwhile, on the scholarship front, things were about to get a lot more interesting. I was nominated for the Schulich Leader Scholarship that year, a nomination capped at one student per school. It had been on my radar for a long time, so even the nomination alone felt genuinely exciting, before I had any idea how it would turn out.

I came across an opportunity that turned into one of the most memorable stretches of senior year, even if, in the end, it wasn't the moment that decided anything for me. I found it on Reddit, of all places, about a week before the deadline. The [Wolf Scholars](https://www.wolfscholars.com/) Program is a fully funded, four-year undergraduate cohort at the University of Toronto's [Faculty of Arts & Science](https://www.artsci.utoronto.ca/), covering tuition, a living stipend, and leadership training through the [Munk School of Global Affairs & Public Policy](https://munkschool.utoronto.ca/). It's open to high school seniors with a 90% average and strong community impact. The odds were brutal: roughly 1,100 applicants narrowed to 92 interviewees, then 32 finalists, then 16 winners. I was eligible because I'd applied to [Computer Science](https://web.cs.toronto.edu/) at UofT, and with a week left on the clock and nothing to lose, I applied anyway.

The written application itself was one of the more thoughtfully built ones I encountered all year, an interface that was actually pleasant to use, and questions that skipped the usual scholarship script entirely. If you're applying to something like this yourself, my honest advice is to reflect on who you actually are, the experiences that shaped you, and how you plan to bring your specific personality into the room to create value, not just credentials.

## December-February

Around this time, Tech Club started losing steam. I'd leaned into lighter topics like [Scratch](https://scratch.mit.edu/) game development early in the year, thinking it would widen our appeal, and it backfired. Attendance dropped, and I had to actually sit with why. Over winter break, I built out a set of Arduino projects: a traffic light system, binary counters, a small sound system. When the club came back in January, it was an instant turnaround, our highest attendance yet. I started recording highlights and posting them to our Instagram, [@bvhstechclub](https://www.instagram.com/bvhstechclub/), if you want to see what those meetings actually looked like. The lesson stuck with me longer than almost anything else from that year: understanding what your audience actually wants matters just as much as knowing what you want to teach them. Software had gotten boring to people. Hardware hadn't. So I adjusted, learned enough to teach it properly, and made it work.

At the beginning of January, I was accepted into McGill.

I was shortlisted for interviews with the Wolf Scholars team in the same month. I spent part of winter break practicing since this was the highest-stakes interview I'd had to that point, and then genuinely had a blast once I got there. It felt more like a real conversation than an evaluation, ranging from my own application to actual geopolitics.

Most university applications closed too, including Waterloo's, which marked the end of the first phase of senior year. What followed was the longer, quieter part: waiting, on both fronts at once.

At the end of the month, I was accepted into UBC and UofT, an exciting way to end the month.

I was selected as a Wolf Scholars finalist. I hadn't expected to make it that far, and the shock of it carried me into a few days in Toronto at the end of February: touring the university, meeting experts in nuclear technology, global affairs, and psychology, wandering the city, and building real connections with people I'd likely never have met otherwise. One more day of interviews followed the same advice as before, be authentic, actually reflect. I didn't win, and for a few days afterward, that genuinely stung, especially having made it that far. But I met 31 other genuinely impressive finalists, walked away with a far broader view of where things are headed globally, and the disappointment faded faster than I expected, mostly because the experience itself had already been worth everything it cost me.

## March

By the beginning of March, the acute stress of applications had faded into something quieter: the slow, sinking realization that my days left in this school were numbered, and shrinking fast. Classes kept their normal rhythm, but I finally found something like balance between coursework, extracurriculars, and an actual social life.

Then, in late March, during lunch hour, sitting in one of my own classrooms, I logged into the application portal the way I had a dozen times before, and saw the "Welcome Warrior!" banner. There was no dramatic build-up to it, just me and a laptop during lunch, but the second it registered, it hit me all at once: three years of a genuinely deliberate plan had actually worked. I sat there for a minute, overwhelmed with gratitude for every person and every opportunity that had gotten me to that exact second, and just let it land.

Writing this now, months later, I still can't fully separate that feeling from everything that led up to it. I'm not sure I could tell you which of those three years mattered most. Grade 9 built the instinct, Grade 10 built the confidence, Grade 11 was mostly just survived, and Grade 12 finally cashed it all in, but none of them feel optional in hindsight. They all feel necessary in a way I genuinely couldn't see while I was living through them.

## April

With that settled, the rest of senior year started to feel lighter, more like enjoying what remained instead of chasing what came next. In Tech Club, we moved from Arduino to Artificial Intelligence: its uses, its ethical questions, building a basic model together in Python and Jupyter Notebooks. Members were genuinely split on whether they even wanted to engage with AI given the concerns around it, which felt like exactly the right note to end the club's run on, given everything happening in the field right now.

I wrote the [Euclid](https://cemc.uwaterloo.ca/contests/euclid) contest, which is one of the recommended contests to write in your final year of high school as it can increase your chances of being accepted into the university as well as your chances of receiving scholarships. My results were underwhelming but this was the last contest I would write before graduating, so it felt like a significant milestone for that reason.

I went back to Mascot Showdown, this time from the other side of it. Instead of just watching from the crowd like the year before, I was recruited to run stand-up comedy during the judges' deliberation period, and leaned hard into the corniest school-specific jokes I could come up with on the spot.

I received news about the Schulich Leader: I'd won it, tied to an acceptance and offer from McGill. It was an incredible offer, genuinely one of the best things that happened to me all year, and turning it down wasn't an easy call in the moment it actually had to be made. But sitting with it now, I don't think it was ever really a fair fight. I'd spent three years building an entire plan around Waterloo's co-op program specifically, so when the actual decision arrived, it had basically already been made for me, years earlier, by a version of myself who didn't know McGill would ever be on the table. That's a strange thing to realize: how much of what feels like a hard choice in the moment was actually decided a long time before you got there.

I led Code in Place one more time that spring, another six weeks teaching Python fundamentals to a new cohort.

## May-June

By May, though, lightness gave way to something else entirely. The reality of graduating had settled over the whole class, and going out of province made it hit differently for me than for most people around me. I'd spent 13 years surrounded by the same people, and a lot of the closest bonds among them came from both sides of that time, the ordinary stuff of just hanging around each other for years, but also the kind of friendship that forms fast when you're all scrambling to fix something twenty minutes before doors open. The idea of no longer sharing a building with any of them the way I had for over a decade felt strange in a way I hadn't fully sat with until then. That realization made me deliberate about spending real time with the people I actually cared about in those final months, because I knew I wouldn't get that time back.

In June, we held our last Tech Club meeting, looking back at how we started, everything in between, and where we'd ended up. Having led the club for three straight years, watching it close was quietly emotional in a way I wasn't fully prepared for. I handed leadership off to a new team before we wrapped for good.

The rest of the year unfolded in the usual senior traditions: Superlatives, Prom, and Senior Sunset, which might be the one I remember most clearly out of all of them. We didn't really do anything structured that night, just sat around as a group, talked, and took an absurd number of photos, which ended up being the only real evidence any of us have that we were even there. A fitting, slightly chaotic way to close out a few years that never really slowed down.

And then I graduated on a random Tuesday. Afterward, everyone congregated out in the parking lot the way you do, nobody quite ready to leave yet. But slowly, in twos and threes, people actually started leaving, and I remember just standing there watching it happen, realizing I probably wouldn't see most of these people again. That was the moment it actually hit me, more than the ceremony itself, more than the diploma. Just a parking lot, emptying out one person at a time. That was really it.

# Comments & Advice

You'll probably notice this post doesn't spell out a formula for getting into a competitive program like Waterloo's. That's intentional. There isn't one fixed path, and there's no single extracurricular that makes an application. If I had to boil down what actually helped: be kind to people and try to lift them up when you can, work hard at whatever you actually commit to, and stay curious even when it's inconvenient. That combination helps, genuinely, but it's not a guarantee, and I want to be honest about that. Waterloo Software Engineering has around 125 seats and had a little over 1,000 applicants last year, which means plenty of strong, talented people get turned away through no real fault of their own. I'm not saying that to discourage you. Apply anyway. Just make sure whatever you send in actually sounds like you, and not like a version of you built to impress someone you've never met.

A few practical notes, since some of this is worth having in one place:
- **Ontario applications** go through the Ontario Universities' Application Centre (OUAC), not the university websites directly. Check your email constantly once you've applied.
- **Waterloo's AIF and engineering interview questions** are public on their website. Use them to start drafting early, even roughly.
- **Info sessions** (first year, co-op, residence) are run in the evenings and are genuinely worth attending if you're serious about the school.
- **Running events with an inexperienced team** works best with a simple structure: pick the event, lock a date three-plus weeks out, assign roles early, finish logistics five days ahead, and plan setup and teardown explicitly, then actually reflect afterward on what worked. It won't run perfectly, and that's fine.

The other reason I wrote this was to actually reflect on my time in high school, not just document it for the sake of a scholarship file or university application. I can honestly say the last three years have been the best of my life so far, even though not everything went right along the way. What made it worth it was the people I met, the things I got to build with my own hands, and the opportunities I never imagined I'd be part of when I was that Grade 9 student who just wanted to get home and tinker with something.

If you're standing where I was three years ago, wondering whether any of it will amount to something, my answer is yes, it genuinely does. No single club, competition, or project guarantees some incredible outcome, but each one teaches you something, introduces you to someone new, or nudges you toward an opportunity you never saw coming. There wasn't ever one defining moment for me. It was an accumulation of hundreds of small ones, each one quietly opening the door to the next, in a way I could only really see clearly once I looked back on all of it at once. More than anything else, I hope this post reminds me, years from now, to never stop saying yes to the next opportunity!
